# A4 / Clause · Circular — a gate where no spend bound may exist without citing the clause that authorises it

**Judged:** 2026-08-26 · **Panel:** IdeaAgent · **Round:** 01 · **Track:** 01
**Note:** two independent idea agents converged on this from different entry points (`A4` from Razorpay-first, `05 · CLAUSE` from measurement-first). Judged as one idea, using the stronger **Clause** framing — the linter embedded in a runtime authorisation gate, not a standalone linter.

---

## One-line pitch
An agentic-payment authorisation gate in which no numeric spend limit may exist in the code unless it carries a machine-checkable citation to the regulatory clause it came from — and a conformance runner that fails the build when the code and the cited clause disagree.

## Problem
Constraint claims drift from the regulation authorising them, and nothing checks. Razorpay's own ACP SEP #216 states *"NPCI's UPI Circle specification imposes a hard **per-transaction** limit"* of ₹15,000. Per NPCI OC No.201/2024-25 §7, ₹15,000 is the **monthly** cap and per-transaction is **₹5,000** — wrong by 3×. The same document asserts *"merchant category restrictions"* that exist nowhere in the NPCI corpus, contradicts itself two tables later, and cites two dead URLs. It was reviewed by Stripe and Meta and sat open for four months. The bound *looked* principled while being wrong.

## User
Any team shipping agentic payments on Indian rails; and, pointedly, a PSP's own protocol team.

## Solution
A `bounds.yaml` where every limit is `{value, unit, scope, source_doc, source_clause, retrieved, checksum}`. A conformance runner re-reads the cited primary source, extracts the governing sentence, and asserts the code's value **and scope** (per-transaction vs monthly vs per-delegation vs first-24h) match. Mismatch fails the build. The gate then enforces those bounds at authorisation time with a distinct refusal code per clause, ahead of `create_order`.

## AI role
**Clause extraction and scope classification from scanned regulatory documents.** Given OC-201 §7's single sentence containing two limits with different scopes, the model must return `{₹5,000, per_transaction}` and `{₹15,000, per_month, per_delegation}` — the exact discrimination Razorpay's own engineers got wrong. Text→structure over adversarial legal prose. **Deliberately not AI:** the comparison, the assertion, the runtime gate, and the verdict. A human-reviewed `APPROVED` checksum must exist before a bound is usable. **Stated cost of that choice:** a newly published circular cannot auto-apply; there is a stale-bound window, which is measured and reported.

## Razorpay role
The rule set *is* Razorpay's operating envelope — UPI Circle and Reserve Pay bounds, UPI AutoPay AFA thresholds, test-mode order creation under those bounds. It addresses a live defect in Razorpay's own open-source standards contribution, and it is the direct instantiation of Razorpay's stated thesis: *"verification capacity, not generation speed, is the bottleneck."*

## Competitors
None found doing this. `aryanpajnee/RazorpayBuildathon` is adjacent but not overlapping — it occupies the mandate/signature layer; this contests whether the numbers *inside* mandates are true. `SweetieBirdX/Kinora` (natural-language licensing terms → machine-enforced gates) is the closest prior art in the field and is not about regulation.

## Judge reaction

**First 10 seconds.** "Every limit in the code has to cite the circular it came from, and CI checks it." A beat of silence, then interest. This is not a pitch anyone else made today. Mild risk that a judge hears "compliance tool" and disengages — the pitch must lead with the *error*, not the mechanism.

**First 60 seconds.** Problem: crisp and verifiable. User: clear. Why-now: excellent — Indian agentic payments are being specified right now, on a misquoted number. Solution: clear. The one thing to watch is that at 60 seconds a judge may still be unsure whether this is a product or a script.

**After demo.** Remembered, verbatim and for the rest of the day: *"he pointed it at Razorpay's own PR and the build went red."* This is the single most memorable artifact across all six ideas, and it is memorable for a reason that flatters the builder's judgement rather than his tooling.

**Deliberation.**

> **Judge C (hackathon):** The build going red on a real public PR. That's the moment of the day.
> **Judge A (product):** It made me uncomfortable and I think that's the point. He didn't say "your engineer was wrong." He said "nothing in the world would have caught this, including us." That's a systems argument.
> **Judge B (senior eng):** Let me try to break the number. Sixty to eighty limit statements from real NPCI and RBI circulars, hand-labelled, scored on exact match of value *and* unit *and* scope jointly. Where's the leakage?
> **Judge D:** There isn't an obvious one. The features are raw scanned text. The labels are read off primary documents. There's no engineered feature that could restate the label.
> **Judge B:** Then the attack is the labeller. One person wrote both the extractor and the labels. Same understanding, same blind spots. If he misreads a clause, he misreads it in both places and scores himself correct.
> **Judge D:** He publishes blind re-labelling agreement on a twenty percent sample.
> **Judge B:** Self-agreement, not inter-annotator agreement. It measures his consistency, not his correctness. It's a real mitigation and it's a partial one — and to his credit he says so.
> **Judge A:** He has one external check that's worth more than all of it: his system disagrees with a *published* document by a third party, and the document is the one that's wrong. That's an out-of-sample hit.
> **Judge D (AI judgment):** And the baseline table. "First rupee figure in the sentence" is the naive approach and it *reproduces the actual bug that shipped.* I've not seen a baseline row do that before. It proves the model earns its place instead of asserting it.
> **Judge B:** Fine. My real worry isn't the metric, it's whether he finished. Scanned circulars, a 403 on the source site, OCR, eighty hand labels, plus a gate, plus an agent, in eight days. I'd expect to receive three of those five things.
> **Judge C:** That's the honest risk. But if it lands, what is it competing with? Everything else today measured something it wrote itself.
> **Judge E (chair):** It's the only submission whose ground truth exists independently of the submitter. Note that.

**Survives deliberation — the strongest of the six.** The surviving objection is *delivery risk*, not conceptual soundness, which is the best kind of objection to be left with ten days out.

## Strongest advantage
**It is the only one of the six ideas whose measurement target it did not create.** The labels come from primary regulatory documents written by NPCI and RBI. Its adversarial baseline does not merely underperform — it *reproduces a bug that actually shipped in the real world*, which is the single most persuasive table row available to anyone in this field. And its LLM is doing work that a regex demonstrably cannot, which converts rubric pillar 3 from a liability into an asset.

## Strongest weakness
Delivery. Scanned-image sources behind a 403, OCR, and 60–80 hand labels made by one person in eight days, on top of a gate and an agent. Every other risk here is manageable; this one is arithmetic.

## Fatal risk

> **The strongest reason this idea loses: it does not finish — or it finishes as a linter rather than a product, and reads as a tool in a track that asked for a working agentic-commerce system.**

Two distinct failure modes, both real:

**(a) Scope.** The critical path is: obtain primary PDFs (blocked by 403 → must be snapshotted manually on day 1) → OCR scanned images → segment clauses → hand-label 60–80 statements → build the extractor → build the conformance runner → build the runtime gate → build the agent and the money path → measure → write it up. That is a lot, and the first three steps are the kind that silently consume two days. If the gate and the agent are the parts that get cut, what remains is a regulatory NLP experiment, which is a different and weaker submission.

**(b) Framing.** Track 01 is agentic commerce. A judge who classifies this as "compliance tooling" in the first fifteen seconds will not reclassify it. The Clause framing — the bounds are enforced at authorisation time, immediately before `create_order`, with clause-citing refusal codes — is what makes it a payment system rather than a document analyser, and it must be visible in the first screen of the README and the first thirty seconds of the video.

**Third risk, lower but non-zero.** The panel may include the SEP #216 author. The corpus is right that using this as a gotcha is a bad trade. The framing must be: *the error is not the finding; the absence of any mechanism that would catch it is the finding.* Get this wrong and a technically excellent submission acquires a personal enemy on the panel.

**Fourth risk.** `npci.org.in` 403s programmatic access. If snapshots are not taken on day 1 and checksummed locally, the conformance runner's central premise — re-read the cited source — becomes undemonstrable at judging time.

## Competitor saturation
**LOW.** Nothing in the 261-repo census or the 10-repo shortlist does regulation-cited bounds. The one adjacent piece of prior art (`Kinora`, NL licensing terms → enforced gates) is a different domain. The direct competitor occupies the layer below and does not contest this.

## Verdict: **GO** — and it is the strongest of the six.

**Weakest dimension to fix: feasibility (3/5).** Not by reducing ambition on the metric — the metric is the whole point — but by ruthlessly time-boxing the acquisition phase and by choosing a host product that already exists in skeleton rather than building one from scratch.

## Required improvements

1. **Day 1, before any code: snapshot every primary source locally with checksums.** OC-201, OC-201A, OC-201B, OC-228, RBI Authentication Directions 2025, the e-mandate framework, the ₹100/day reversal circular. If a source cannot be obtained, its bound does not exist. Publish the list of bounds that had to be dropped for lack of a fetchable source — that attrition ledger is itself a credibility asset.
2. **Time-box labelling to one day and report the true n.** Forty clauses honestly labelled with a published attrition ledger beats eighty rushed ones. Report effective n next to the headline, always.
3. **Ship the naive-baseline table on the README's first screen**, with the row that shows "first ₹ figure in sentence" reproducing the SEP #216 error. This is the single highest-value artifact in the submission — do not bury it in a docs folder.
4. **Score on joint `(value, unit, scope)` exact match — never on value alone.** Value-alone is precisely the metric under which the real-world bug would have *passed*. Say that explicitly.
5. **Disclose the single-annotator limitation in the builder's own words**, before a judge raises it, and publish the blind re-label agreement while naming what it does and does not prove.
6. **Make the gate visible, not the linter.** Lead with a money action being refused with a clause citation. The conformance runner is what makes the bound trustworthy; the refusal is what makes it a product.
7. **Frame the SEP finding as a systems argument in every artifact.** Never name an individual. The sentence is: *nothing existed that would have caught this, for four months, in a document reviewed by three payment companies.*
8. **Do not build on UAP.** Cite it as a slot, attribute its status as single-sourced reporting, assert nothing.
